How to Read the Structure of a Lab Report

Before interpreting individual test values, it helps to understand the standard structure of a report from any accredited pathology lab near me. Every well-formatted lab report from our Gani Hospitals laboratory contains the following sections:

Patient Information

Name, age, gender, and patient ID. Always verify this matches your details before reading results — reports are patient-specific.

Sample Details

Date and time of sample collection and report issue. Relevant for fasting tests — confirms whether fasting was observed at the correct time.

Test Name

The name of the investigation performed — for example, Haemoglobin, Serum Creatinine, or TSH. Some reports list the panel name and sub-tests beneath it.

Your Result

The measured value from your sample. This is the number that gets compared against the reference range to determine if it is normal, low, or high.

Unit of Measurement

The unit in which the result is expressed — g/dL, mg/dL, IU/L, mEq/L, and so on. Units matter because the same number means very different things in different units.

Reference Range

The normal range for that test based on age and gender. A result within this range is considered normal. Results outside this range are flagged as H (high) or L (low).

Flags / Remarks

H, L, HH (critically high), or LL (critically low) flags alert the doctor to values requiring attention. Our diagnostic lab in Ramanathapuram also adds interpretive comments for complex results.

Pathologist Signature

The authorising MD pathologist's name and signature confirms the report is validated. This is a mandatory component of every report from our Gani Hospitals laboratory.

Understanding Your Report Flags
Within Range — Result falls within the normal reference range. No action typically required unless symptoms persist.
L — Low — Result is below the lower limit of the reference range. Requires clinical correlation with your doctor.
H — High — Result exceeds the upper limit of the reference range. Degree of elevation determines urgency of follow-up.
LL / HH — Critical Value — Significantly outside safe limits. Our clinical lab Ramnad contacts the referring doctor immediately for all critical values.

Urine Routine Examination — What to Look For

A urine routine examination (urine R/M) is one of the simplest and most informative tests at any pathology lab near me. It screens the kidneys, urinary tract, and metabolic status simultaneously. Here is how to read the key components in your report from our diagnostic lab in Ramanathapuram:

  • Colour and appearance: Normal urine is pale to dark yellow and clear. Cloudy urine may indicate infection. Red or brown urine requires urgent evaluation.
  • pH: Normal range 4.5 to 8.0. Consistently acidic or alkaline urine can indicate metabolic disorders or urinary tract infection.
  • Specific gravity: 1.001 to 1.035. High specific gravity indicates concentrated urine (dehydration). Low specific gravity may suggest diabetes insipidus or kidney disease.
  • Protein: Should be absent or trace. Persistent proteinuria (protein in urine) is a key early marker of kidney damage — particularly important in diabetic and hypertensive patients monitored at our Gani Hospitals laboratory.
  • Glucose: Should be absent in normal urine. Glucose in urine (glycosuria) occurs when blood glucose exceeds the renal threshold — approximately 180 mg/dL.
  • Ketones: Absent normally. Present in diabetic ketoacidosis, prolonged fasting, or starvation. Requires urgent medical review when found alongside high blood glucose.
  • Blood / RBCs: Absent normally. Blood in urine requires further evaluation for kidney stones, urinary tract infection, bladder pathology, or renal disease through our pathology services Ramnad.
  • WBCs / Pus cells: 0 to 5 per high-power field is normal. More than 5 pus cells indicates urinary tract infection requiring culture and sensitivity testing.
  • Casts: Granular, hyaline, or red cell casts indicate varying degrees of kidney disease and require specialist nephrology review.
  • Crystals: Uric acid or calcium oxalate crystals in urine indicate predisposition to kidney stone formation — particularly relevant in Ramanathapuram's coastal population.

Tips for Getting the Most Accurate Lab Results

Even the most advanced diagnostic lab in Ramanathapuram cannot compensate for poor patient preparation. These tips from our Gani Hospitals laboratory pathology team ensure your results are accurate and meaningful:

Fast Correctly

Fast for 10 to 12 hours for glucose, lipid, and liver tests. Continue drinking plain water. Skipping water during the fast makes veins collapse and samples harder to collect.

Declare All Medicines

Inform the phlebotomist and pathologist of every medicine you are taking. Biotin supplements, anticoagulants, and corticosteroids interfere with several test methodologies.

Avoid Exercise Before Tests

Strenuous exercise within 24 hours before your test raises creatine kinase, creatinine, and uric acid — potentially creating false abnormal results in your kidney and muscle enzyme panel.

Avoid Alcohol for 48 Hours

Alcohol transiently elevates GGT, triglycerides, and liver enzymes — common sources of unnecessary follow-up investigation when the cause is simply pre-test alcohol consumption.

Ideal Timing is Morning

Most reference ranges are established based on morning samples after overnight fast. Afternoon collection for fasting tests reduces the reliability of comparison to reference values.

Women: Timing Matters

Hormone tests (LH, FSH, progesterone) are cycle-phase specific. Inform our pathology lab near me team of the day of your cycle when booking reproductive hormone tests.

Bring Previous Reports

Year-on-year trending is more valuable than any single result. Our laboratory test guide Tamil Nadu clinicians use serial values to identify meaningful changes versus normal variation.

Always Discuss With Your Doctor

No single lab value confirms or rules out a diagnosis in isolation. Use this blood test results guide as a foundation — then discuss every flagged result with your treating physician.

When to Contact Your Doctor Urgently About Lab Results

Our clinical lab Ramnad contacts your doctor directly for critical values. However, if you receive a report independently and notice any of the following, seek medical attention the same day:
  • Haemoglobin below 7 g/dL — severe anaemia requiring evaluation and possible transfusion
  • Platelet count below 50,000 — significantly increased bleeding risk; urgent haematology review
  • Blood glucose above 400 mg/dL — risk of diabetic ketoacidosis or hyperosmolar state
  • Serum potassium above 6.0 mEq/L or below 2.5 mEq/L — both are cardiac emergencies
  • Serum sodium below 120 mEq/L or above 155 mEq/L — neurological emergency
  • Serum creatinine above 5.0 mg/dL in a patient not known to have kidney disease — possible acute kidney injury
  • Total bilirubin above 10 mg/dL — severe jaundice requiring hospitalisation assessment
  • INR above 5.0 in a patient on anticoagulants — critical bleeding risk requiring dose adjustment
  • Any marked HH or LL (double critical flag) on your report from our Gani Hospitals laboratory
